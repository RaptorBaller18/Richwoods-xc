Race Prep: Recover Better, PR Better

Our recovery model shows these factors matter most. Check them in the days before a race to give yourself the best chance to PR and move up the leaderboard. Each chart plots one factor against the recovery score, and each dot is one record from the model's data.

Raises your recovery score

  • 1. Heart rate variability (HRV, in ms)Higher HRV means you are recovered. Measure it every morning with the same device and compare to your own baseline, not a teammate's. Steady sleep, easy days and low stress keep it up.
    Recovery score
    HRV (ms)
  • 2. Overall energy levelRate your energy each morning. If it is low two days in a row, make the next workout easy instead of pushing through.
    Recovery score
    Energy level
  • 3. Hours of sleepKeep a consistent bedtime, especially for the 2 to 3 nights before a race. The night before can be restless, so the nights before that matter most.
    Recovery score
    Sleep duration (hours)
  • 4. Mood scoreStress from school and life counts too. Check in with your mood and do something that helps you relax before race week.
    Recovery score
    Mood score

Lowers your recovery score

  • 1. Muscle sorenessSore legs mean you have not recovered yet. Swap a hard day for an easy jog and avoid stacking hard days back to back.
    Recovery score
    Muscle soreness
  • 2. Higher resting heart rateCheck your pulse as soon as you wake up. If it is several beats above your normal, treat that as a sign to back off.
    Recovery score
    Resting heart rate
  • 3. Training intensityHard sessions late in the week cost you on race day. Cut volume and hard workouts in the final week and keep only short, easy pickups.

About the model

The model estimates an athlete's recovery score. Recovery is a stepping stone to better racing: it shows how recovered and ready an athlete is, and it does not predict race times or PRs directly.

What it predicts

How recovered an athlete is. That recovery score can then be used to see how ready an athlete is to train or race.

The recovery score

A comprehensive score from 0.0 to 100.0 that indicates the athlete's overall physical readiness and recovery.

Where the data came from

The model was trained on the public Athlete Recovery and Biometric Performance dataset on Kaggle: 300 athletes tracked over 28 days. It is not data from Richwoods athletes.

Limits to keep in mind

  • These are patterns in the data, not proof that one factor causes another.
  • Energy, mood, soreness and (often) training intensity are self-rated, so they are subjective.
  • The recovery score stops at 100, and the model is less precise near that cap.
  • The athletes in the data were ages 18 to 41 across different sports, not high school cross country runners, so results may differ for your team.

How reliable is the model?

The recovery model is not available to run on this page, so here is how it performed when tested on records it had not seen during training (cross validation).

7.6points
Average miss

On the 0 to 100 recovery score, predictions were off by about 8 points on average (±0.15 between test splits).

9.6points
Typical error (RMSE)

The same idea, but bigger misses count more. It stays under 10 points (±0.19).

88%
Differences explained

The squared correlation is 0.882 (±0.005), so the model captures about 88% of the ups and downs in recovery score.

The very small ± values mean the scores stayed steady from one test split to the next. A prediction is a guide, not a guarantee.

How the rankings work

  • Boys results are compared only with the boys worksheet; girls results are compared only with the girls worksheet.
  • All-time rankings count recorded performances. The Top 10 lists show each runner once, using their fastest recorded performance.
  • Race-specific rankings use the workbook's Race column. A dash is shown for your own rank in comparison tables.
  • Training paces are the same for boys and girls and come from the Mega List. The 3-mile time is matched to the closest goal time at or faster than it (for example, 15:25 uses the 15:15 paces); anything slower than 30:00 uses the 30:00 paces.
